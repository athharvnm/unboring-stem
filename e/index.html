Calculators & Compilers — Computing e^x Using Only +, −, ×, ÷

Microprocessors do not natively know what an irrational constant like e or e^x is—they execute basic arithmetic. Using the Taylor series e^x = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …, software computes exponentials to full precision in just a handful of steps.

Key insight: Because factorials in the denominator explode (12! = 479,001,600), the remainder shrinks faster than almost any other series.

Why Does the Average Equal e?

The probability that k independent uniform random numbers in [0, 1] sum to less than 1 is 1 / k!.

Summing those probabilities across all k gives the expected number of draws: 1 + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + … = e!

Euler’s Identity & Nature’s Blueprint

Multiply an exponent by the imaginary unit i and growth turns into pure rotation around the unit circle: eiθ = cos(θ) + i·sin(θ).

Tap a Piece of e^(iθ) = cos(θ) + i·sin(θ) e^(i·0.79) = 0.71 + 0.71i
e^(iθ) — "Start at 1, turn by angle θ, stay on radius 1"

Normally e^t stretches along a straight number line. Putting i (a 90° turn) in the exponent turns stretching into spinning around a circle of radius 1 by angle θ (in radians).

High School Intuition Menu
1. Why "i" Makes e Spin in a Circle — Turning Growth into Turning
e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ

Think of a normal power like e^t as stretching along a straight line. In complex numbers, multiplying by i means making a 90° left turn. Put i in the exponent and instead of growing bigger and bigger along a line, you walk around a circle of radius 1!

Think of it this way: Regular exponents change how big you are; imaginary exponents change which direction you face. (Angle θ = radians around circle)

100°C Start & 20°C Room — The 80°C Temperature Gap

Boiling coffee starts at 100°C in a 20°C room. That gives an 80°C extra heat gap above room temperature—and as time t passes, that extra gap shrinks smoothly toward 0°C above the room (so coffee settles at 20°C).

Cooling & Decay Intuition Menu
1. Coffee at 100°C — Drops Fast at First, Slows Near 20°C
T(t) = 20 + 80 · e^(-kt)

At t = 0, your coffee is boiling at 100°C in a 20°C room—an 80°C gap. Because the gap is huge at first, heat escapes fast; as it nears 20°C, cooling slows way down and never drops below room temperature.

Everyday rule: Bigger gap or bigger k means faster cooling; near 20°C it barely changes. Newton’s law is an approximation; an open cup typically has k ≈ 1.5–2 per hour. (Starts at 100°C → settles at 20°C)
