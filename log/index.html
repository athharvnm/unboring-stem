Step 1 · Motivation Before Formulas

Why do we even need logarithms?

Imagine trying to draw an ant (0.001 m), a person (1.7 m), Mount Everest (8,849 m), and Planet Earth (diameter ≈ 12,742,000 m) on one paper ruler. On a standard linear ruler, everything smaller than a continent gets crushed into a single dot near zero. Logarithmic scales give every 10× jump equal space on the ruler.

Start with 1 unit and multiply by the same positive factor b (with b > 0, b ≠ 1) repeatedly. For a whole number of steps k, logb(bk) = k asks: "How many multiplication steps did it take to reach our target?"

Step 3 · Connecting Exponents to Logarithms

Logarithms are the inverse ("undo") operation for exponentials

Addition has subtraction; multiplication has division; for a fixed base b > 0 (b ≠ 1), the exponential map x ↦ bx and logarithm x ↦ logb(x) undo each other on their domains: logb(bx) = x for all real x, and blogb(u) = u for all u > 0.

Domain Restriction: Base cannot be 1!

If b = 1, then 1^x = 1 for every real x, so it is not one-to-one and cannot be inverted to define log_1(u).

Why Coordinates Flip Across y = x (0, 1) ↔ (1, 0) · (1, 2) ↔ (2, 1)

Swapping a coordinate pair (a, bcoord) to (bcoord, a) reflects it across the diagonal line y = x. Because b0 = 1 and b1 = b, the points (0, 1) and (1, b) on the exponential curve reflect to (1, 0) and (b, 1) on the logarithm curve.

Why Base 10 and Base 2 are everyday heroes: Base-10 logarithms (log10(x)) track decimal orders of magnitude (102 = 100, 103 = 1,000), while base-2 logarithms (log2(x)) measure binary scales and information bits (28 = 256).
Step 4 · Nature's Continuous Clock

What if growth doesn't wait until the end of the year?

Physical, biological, and financial models often describe change continuously over time rather than in one annual jump. If $1 grows at a hypothetical 100% nominal annual rate (100% interest? Good luck finding a bank that generous, unless your cousin runs it!) compounded in n equal periods per year, the balance at year-end is (1 + 1/n)n. As n → ∞, this sequence converges monotonically to Euler's number e ≈ 2.718281828459045.

Splitting a 100% nominal rate into n equal periods gives 1/n per period. Each period multiplies the current balance by (1 + 1/n), compounding prior gains too.

Step 5 · Algebraic Identities of Logarithms

Turn multiplication, division, and powers into simpler operations

Because bu+v = bubv, taking logb converts products into sums: for positive numbers A, B > 0 and any real k, logb(AB) = logb(A) + logb(B), logb(A/B) = logb(A) − logb(B), and logb(Ak) = k·logb(A).

The 5 Big Takeaways You Now Know

1. Scale

Taming Huge Ranges

Logarithmic scales map multiplicative 10× jumps to additive spacing (decibels, earthquake magnitude scales, and pH = −log10[H+]).

2. Intuition

Counting Steps

For integer powers, log2(32) = 5 counts how many times you multiply 1 by 2 to reach 32.

3. Inverse

The Inverse Map

For b > 0, b ≠ 1, by = x (with x > 0) is equivalent to logb(x) = y, reflecting across y = x.

4. Base e

Continuous Growth

e ≈ 2.71828 is the limit of (1 + 1/n)n as n → ∞, and ln(u) is loge(u) for u > 0.

5. Laws

Products to Sums

logb(AB) = logb(A) + logb(B) for A, B > 0. Adding logarithmic lengths corresponds to multiplying values.
